Architecture

A meeting app that doesn’t eat your phone’s battery.

Why VisualTalk is built on Rust and Tauri instead of Electron — and why your phone notices first.

In plain words

  • Your battery: when a meeting ends, the app truly goes to sleep. Nothing keeps working in the background, so between calls it costs your battery nothing.
  • Your storage: the whole app is a small download — a few megabytes, roughly two or three photos — and the only things it keeps are the ones you save yourself, like recordings you chose to make or notes you typed.
  • During a call: it borrows only the power and memory the call itself needs, and gives it all back the moment you hang up.

On a phone, every megabyte resident in RAM and every background millisecond comes straight out of your battery. So VisualTalk ships no browser engine of its own — it draws its interface in the webview Android and iOS already provide — and it runs nothing at all between calls: no sync daemon, no push SDK, no telemetry beacon. The logic that matters lives in a small Rust core. The result is longer calls, a cooler phone, and a battery graph you can check yourself in your phone’s settings.

Your phone’s battery, first

  • Nothing runs between calls. The Android manifest declares no services, no broadcast receivers and no push SDK: when you leave a meeting, VisualTalk stops. Check it yourself — Android: Settings → Apps → VisualTalk → Battery; iOS: Settings → Battery. Outside calls, the number stays where it was.
  • No second browser in your RAM. Bundling a browser engine means paying for its memory and its background scheduling on top of the one your phone already runs. VisualTalk draws its interface in the system WebView on Android and WKWebView on iOS — shared with the operating system, updated with it, and already warm.
  • The wake lock exists only during a call. The single WAKE_LOCK permission is the media-session lock that keeps audio alive if your screen sleeps mid-call; it is released when the meeting ends. Nothing keeps your phone awake behind your back.
  • Hardware does the heavy media work. Audio and video ride the platform’s own WebRTC stack and its hardware codecs — the same accelerated path the system browser uses — while signaling, tokens and, in Pro, end-to-end encryption run as native Rust instead of JavaScript competing for the main thread.
  • Small enough to forget. The store delivers only the slice for your device’s architecture, and there is no engine of ours to update beside the OS’s own. The Android app is in closed testing on Google Play and the iOS app is with Apple for review; desktop ships today.

The same logic on your laptop

  • Your RAM stays yours. A browser-bundling client carries its own copy of Chromium plus the helper processes it spawns. VisualTalk renders in the webview the OS already loads — WebKit on macOS, WebView2 on Windows, WebKitGTK on Linux — so there is no second browser sitting resident in memory beside the one you already have open.
  • Idle actually means idle. With no Node.js runtime to keep warm between clicks and no browser engine scheduling background work, an open VisualTalk window costs close to nothing while you are listening or talking — the desktop version of the same battery argument.
  • A download measured in megabytes. The Windows installer is 4.2 MiB and the macOS disk image 6.3 MiB, where browser-bundling desktop apps typically run 100–200 MB. The one exception is the Linux AppImage (83.7 MiB), which packs its own WebKitGTK because we cannot assume your distro has one — use the .deb or .rpm (7.7 MiB) if it does.

The Rust advantage: thin UI, fat core

Our design rule is thin UI, fat core. The interface is a small SolidJS layer that renders state and forwards your clicks; everything that decides, signs, encrypts or persists lives in Rust behind an explicit command boundary. Your LiveKit API secret, for instance, is held and used in the Rust core and never enters the webview’s JavaScript heap.

  • Memory safety without a garbage collector. Rust’s ownership rules make use-after-free, buffer overruns and data races impossible at compile time — the family of bugs that otherwise reaches users as an unexplained crash mid-meeting.
  • Native speed where it counts. Token signing, session authority, window lifecycle, persistence and — in Pro — end-to-end encryption (ECDH P-256 key agreement, a fresh AES-256-GCM key for every message) run as compiled native code, on a phone as on a laptop.
  • Quieter hardware, one codebase. Less background churn means fewer fan spin-ups on a long call and less thermal throttling on a phone, and the same Rust core drives macOS, Windows, Linux, iOS and Android — so a fix or a feature lands everywhere at once.

What we are not claiming

We have not published a benchmark suite, so we won’t invent one: no “X% more battery” figure appears on this page, and an app that promises you one for video calls is selling something. The largest battery costs in any call — the screen, the radio, encoding and decoding video — are paid by every client, ours included. What we remove is everything around them: a bundled engine, background processes, and JavaScript doing a native core’s job. The mobile facts above are checkable in your own phone’s settings; the desktop sizes are downloadable artifacts you can verify.
